## Historical Editor's Note

This model issue treats 2025 as a bridge year. It is close enough to the contemporary quarterly review that the archive can still be checked against living journal records, yet far enough from the present issue workflow to demonstrate the historical program's rule: reconstruct the field as a legal philosopher would have seen it at the time, and mark later perspective as later perspective.

The issue's center is orthodox legal philosophy, not AI law. The core questions are Hartian and post-Hartian: What makes legal content legal content? What sort of reasons does law purport to give? How does authority operate when law is both a social practice and a normative practice? What survives of the Hart-Dworkin debate once the field has absorbed Raz, Finnis, Fuller, Alexy, Habermas, Luhmann, Postema, Hohfeldian analysis, private law theory, and rule-of-law theory?

AI appears only because it became a visible jurisprudential pressure point in leading legal philosophy venues. In 2025, *Law and Philosophy* published "The Death of the Legal Author: Authority, Intention, and Law-Creation in the Advent of GenAI," a paper explicitly about legal authority, legal authorship, intention, and legal interpretation.[^rabanos-spaic] That makes AI admissible here, but not central. The deeper question is older: can law be explained by authoritative sources without reducing law to text, intention, official power, or moral truth?

## Field Map of 2025

### 1. The Sources Thesis Did Not Disappear

The field still begins with Hart. The standard orientation remains that legal positivism makes the existence and content of law depend on social facts rather than moral merit.[^sep-positivism] But by 2025 the interesting work no longer lay in merely repeating the separation thesis. The live question was how social facts become legal content in an argumentative practice.

This is why several 2025 pieces matter together. *Legal Theory* published "Legal Perspectivalism and Hartian Orthodoxy," which tests whether Hartian validation by a rule of recognition can coexist with claims about legal rights understood as moral rights according to law.[^ryu-sewell] In the same volume, "Can We Legally Revise the Highest Legal Rule?" returns to Hart and Alf Ross on whether the highest rule can be changed by legal reasoning rather than by extra-legal revolution.[^oza] These are not museum pieces in Hart studies. They show that the ultimate rule, internal point of view, and social recognition remain pressure points for contemporary jurisprudence.

The 2025 field therefore looked less like a post-positivist landscape than a post-slogan positivist landscape. Social facts remained indispensable, but their shape was contested. Are they official practices, shared plans, social norms, conventions, interpretive convergences, or institutionally mediated reason-giving structures? A serious reader in 2025 had to read Hart not as a settled answer but as a grammar for formulating the next problem.

### 2. Razian Authority Remained a Live Center

Raz's authority theory continued to organize disputes about reasons, exclusion, and the law's claim to bind. *Law and Philosophy* Volume 44 included "The Sources of Authoritative Exclusion," which directly revisits exclusionary reasons and the normal justification thesis.[^coyne] The same volume later published "Challenging the State's Claim to Authority," which argues that authority should be understood dynamically through claiming, challenging, and responding, not only through de jure legitimacy.[^kenneth-yarom]

This matters because the Razian inheritance pulls jurisprudence toward practical reason. The question is not merely whether a norm has legal pedigree. The question is what kind of practical situation is created when an institution claims that its directive should replace or pre-empt the subject's own deliberation. In 2025, the authority debate was not over. It had moved from "does law claim legitimate authority?" to "what must social and political power look like for that claim to be intelligible?"

### 3. Dworkin's Question Survived Dworkin's Answers

Dworkinian interpretivism remained unavoidable because legal content still appeared argumentative. SEP's account of legal interpretation and coherence continues to frame Dworkin's central thought: legal interpretation seeks principles that fit and justify legal practice.[^sep-interpretation] But 2025 did not simply replay *Law's Empire*. It treated Dworkin's question as separable from Dworkin's full moralized answer.

That is visible in work on legislative intent, legal authorship, and hard legal content. *Law and Philosophy* published "Legislative Intent and the Hard Problem of Content" in 2025.[^lawphil-44-3] Its placement beside papers on authoritative exclusion and legal authorship is revealing. The field was asking how content arises when texts, intentions, institutions, and interpretive practices fail to line up neatly.

This is also where Jeyoun Son's 2025 article, "The Interpretive Convergence Theory of Law," belongs as a useful regional and theoretical comparator.[^son2025] Its claim, in brief, is that legal norms arise through interpretive convergence around institutional acts. It should not be presented as already central in Anglophone 2025 reception. Its visibility at the time was limited by venue and language. But conceptually it sits near the live question: how do authoritative acts become legal meaning through uptake, discourse, and institutional practice?

### 4. Rights, Standing, and Hohfeld Returned as Analytical Infrastructure

The 2025 legal philosophy field was not only about the nature of law. It was also about the grammar of normative positions. *Legal Theory*'s 2025 Oxford Seminars in Jurisprudence special issue included papers on moral standing, state standing to hold marginalized offenders to account, rights and remedies, youth and punishment, and directed wrongdoing.[^legaltheory-osj] *Law and Philosophy* published Luís Duarte d'Almeida's "Normative Positions: Against the Dual View," a direct intervention in the Hohfeldian analysis of duties, liberties, claim-rights, powers, liabilities, and immunities.[^duarte]

That cluster is important. Hohfeldian analysis looks dry only until one notices that nearly every substantive dispute in rights theory, private law, criminal responsibility, and public law depends on whether a party has a claim, liberty, power, immunity, standing, or remedy. In 2025, analytical jurisprudence remained a technology of precision for normative conflict.

### 5. Rule of Law Theory Was Methodologically Self-Conscious

Rule-of-law theory in 2025 had two pressures. One was political: democratic backsliding, emergency power, platform governance, disinformation, and institutional distrust. The other was methodological: why do theorists remain deadlocked about the rule of law's concept, value, and relation to democracy, rights, legality, and justice?

Tom Hannant's "Deadlock in Rule of Law Theory and the Potential of Internal Critique" is a useful marker because it does not merely add another definition of the rule of law. It diagnoses a methodological problem in theory construction itself.[^hannantrule] Gerald Postema's later rule-of-law work also mattered as a background text. His account of law's rule emphasizes fidelity, reflexivity, and mutual accountability across a political community, not merely formal constraint on officials.[^postema-lawsrule] A reader in 2025 could therefore see rule-of-law theory moving away from checklist formalism toward social practices of accountability.

### 6. European and German Legal Theory Were Present, But Not Always on Their Own Terms

Anglo-American journals in 2025 continued to absorb European and German legal theory unevenly. Alexy's rights theory and proportionality analysis remained central for constitutional rights discourse.[^alexy] Habermas remained indispensable for law, democracy, legitimacy, and discourse theory.[^habermas] Luhmann continued to matter for systems-theoretical accounts of legal autonomy and legal communication.[^luhmann]

But the field visible from English-language journals often treated these traditions as resources rather than as autonomous intellectual worlds. A serious historical issue must therefore separate two claims. Time-situated claim: Anglo-American legal philosophy in 2025 frequently used European and German concepts through the lenses of proportionality, rights, constitutionalism, legitimacy, and systems. Retrospective editorial caution: this does not exhaust German public law theory or continental legal philosophy as they appeared in their own languages, institutions, and journals.

## Journal and Book Coverage Report

### Journals

Three journal records were especially useful for this model issue.

First, *Legal Theory* Volume 31 shows a field still interested in punishment, standing, remedies, precedent, Hartian orthodoxy, the rule of law, political obligation, and highest legal rules. Its 2025 Oxford Seminars in Jurisprudence special issue is not a narrow "nature of law" issue, but it displays the breadth of jurisprudence as moral, legal, and political philosophy.[^legaltheory-all]

Second, *Law and Philosophy* Volume 44 is unusually helpful for the issue's thesis. It includes articles on authoritative exclusion, GenAI and legal authorship, Bentham/Hart/Postema, state authority, Hohfeldian normative positions, tort law independence, property and authority, hate speech and liberalism, rights, discrimination, and refugee policy.[^lawphil-vol44] This is raw evidence that authority, reasons, rights, legal content, and institutional legitimacy were live in a leading venue.

Third, *Oxford Journal of Legal Studies* Volume 45 confirms that generalist top legal scholarship remained hospitable to legal-philosophical work. Its 2025 Winter issue includes David Enoch's "Law, Philosophy and the Susceptible Skins of Living Beings," rights of nature, animal agency, electoral disinformation under the ECHR, mental capacity, tort remoteness, and doctrinal method.[^ojls-45-4] OJLS also presents itself as a refereed generalist journal for scholarship of intellectual value to legal scholars worldwide.[^ojls-about]

## Ten Works to Read in 2025

1. Hart, *The Concept of Law*. Read for the internal point of view, rule of recognition, open texture, and the social-fact grounding of legal validity.

2. Raz, *The Authority of Law*. Read for legal authority, exclusionary reasons, and the legal system's claim to guide action.

3. Dworkin, *Law's Empire*. Read for fit, justification, integrity, and the challenge to source-based theories of legal content.

4. Finnis, *Natural Law and Natural Rights*. Read for practical reasonableness, common good, authority, obligation, and unjust law.

5. Fuller, *The Morality of Law*. Read for legality as an internal morality of law and for the continuing problem of form, fidelity, and governance by rules.

6. Postema, *Legal Philosophy in the Twentieth Century: The Common Law World*. Read for the Holmes-Hart arc and for jurisprudence as historically situated philosophical practice.[^postema-20c]

7. Postema, *Law's Rule*. Read for rule of law as reflexive mutual accountability rather than official rule-following alone.

8. Alexy, *A Theory of Constitutional Rights*. Read for principles, optimization, proportionality, and the European grammar of constitutional rights.

9. Habermas, *Between Facts and Norms*. Read for legitimacy, discourse, democracy, private and public autonomy, and the legal medium.

10. A 2025 journal dossier: Coyne on authoritative exclusion, Ryu and Sewell on Hartian orthodoxy, Oza on revising the highest rule, Hannant on rule-of-law deadlock, Duarte d'Almeida on Hohfeldian positions, Kenneth and Yarom on state authority, Enoch on the practical stakes of legal philosophy, and Rabanos and Spaic on legal authorship in the advent of GenAI.

## Concepts Under Pressure

**Authority.** Raz made authority intelligible through reasons. In 2025, the challenge was to explain authority under contestation: who can challenge the state's claim, what kind of power is presupposed by legal authority, and whether AI-generated legal text can be authoritative without intention.

**Legal content.** The field was still struggling with the relation between text, source, intention, practice, and moral reason. The hard cases were no longer only appellate penumbras. They included legal perspectivalism, constitutional change, inherited legal frameworks, legislative intention, and machine-generated legal drafts.

**Standing.** The Oxford Seminars in Jurisprudence special issue shows standing migrating across moral, criminal, feminist, political, and private-law contexts. Standing became a way of asking who may make a demand, who may hold another accountable, and what defects defeat that power.

**Rule of law.** The rule of law was not only a virtue of legal systems but a contested theoretical object. Formal, procedural, substantive, democratic, and accountability-based accounts continued to compete.

**Rights.** Rights theory in 2025 had both analytic and practical edges: Hohfeldian clarity, discrimination, nature rights, animal agency, freedom of speech, remedies, and constitutional proportionality.

## Retrospective Note from June 2026

From June 2026, the temptation is to make 2025 a generative-AI year. That would be a mistake for this magazine. The better retrospective judgment is narrower and more interesting: 2025 exposed how old jurisprudential questions reappear when legal systems face new forms of text production, institutional distrust, and democratic stress.

AI did not replace Hart, Raz, or Dworkin. It made their questions less optional. If legal text can be produced without legal intention, we need an account of authorship and authority. If citizens face algorithmic or administrative decisions that appear lawful, we need rule-of-law criteria that reach beyond surface regularity. If courts and lawyers confront generated citations and automated reasoning, we need a theory of legal reasons, responsibility, and institutional uptake.

This is also where Son's interpretive convergence account is worth watching. Its importance for this issue is not that it had become globally canonical in 2025. It had not. Its value is that it names a problem shared by positivists and interpretivists: official acts do not become determinate legal meanings merely by occurring, and moral argument does not become law merely by being sound. Law is stabilized through interpretive convergence around institutional acts. That proposal should be tested against Hartian official practice, Razian authority, Dworkinian fit and justification, and Postema's account of fidelity and mutual accountability.

## Teaching Note

A four-week seminar built around this issue should proceed as follows:

1. Week 1: Hart, Raz, and the sources-authority problem. Read Hart, Raz, SEP on legal positivism, Coyne, and Kenneth/Yarom.
2. Week 2: Dworkin, content, and interpretation. Read *Law's Empire*, SEP on interpretivism, Ryu/Sewell, Oza, and the 2025 legal authorship article.
3. Week 3: Rights, standing, and normative positions. Read Hohfeld, Duarte d'Almeida, the Oxford Seminars standing/remedies cluster, and selected rights theory.
4. Week 4: Rule of law, European theory, and future pressure points. Read Fuller, Postema, Alexy, Habermas, Luhmann, Hannant, and Enoch.

The seminar's central question should be: when legal institutions act, what must be added by social practice, practical reason, moral justification, and interpretive uptake before we can say that law has content and authority?

## What Would Have Been Worth Watching Next?

A legally and philosophically informed editor at the end of 2025 should have watched five lines of development.

First, the re-opening of Hartian foundations through legal perspectivalism, social norms, highest-rule revision, and inherited legal frameworks.

Second, the Razian debate after Raz: whether authority is best understood through exclusionary reasons, service, claims to rule, public power, or institutional trust.

Third, rights theory's return to analytical infrastructure: Hohfeld, standing, remedies, discrimination, nature rights, and animal agency.

Fourth, rule-of-law theory's methodological self-critique: whether theorists can move past deadlock without reducing legality to morality, democracy, or administrative regularity.

Fifth, computational legal text as a narrow but serious jurisprudential test case: not "AI and law" as fashion, but authorship, intention, responsibility, reasons, and institutional uptake under conditions of machine-generated language.
